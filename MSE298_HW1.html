The thermoelectric figure of merit is defined as:

$$ zT = \frac{\sigma S^{2} T}{\kappa}$$

where $\sigma$ is the electrical conductivity, S is the Seebeck coefficient, $\kappa$ is the thermal conductivity, and T is the difference in temperature. This is often used to calculate a material's efficiency at converting heat into voltage/power.

As a sanity check, I will plot zT against the tabulated values. Much of the dataset is missing the temperature difference, so I don't expect this to fit well, but for a general linear trend to appear.

from pymatgen.core import Composition
def get_at_percent_dict(formula):
    try:
        comp = Composition(formula)
        # get_el_amt_dict() returns {Element: Stoichiometry}
        # comp.num_atoms is the sum of all coefficients
        return {str(el): (amt / comp.num_atoms) * 100 
                for el, amt in comp.get_el_amt_dict().items()}
    except Exception as e:
        # Useful for catching typos like "Gz" (not an element)
        print(f"Error parsing {formula}: {e}")
        return {}

2.3 Summarize your insights¶

The physical equation for the thermoelectric figure of merit is perfectly conserved, indicating that this is a calculated value rather than a cross measured one:

$$ zT = \frac{\sigma S^{2} T}{\kappa}$$

where in the dataset, $\sigma$ is $\frac{1}{rho (ohm.cm)}$ in the dataset, S is "S [muV/K]", T is "T [K]", and $\kappa$ is "kappa [W/mK]". Since this data is derived from one another, I dropped the constiuent values in favor of highlighting the other variables relationships to zT.

Generally, as the components of PCA 1 and PCA 2 increase, so does zT. There is a strong nonlinear relationship, where high PCA 1 and high PCA 2 does not appear to exist, and the maximum of the properties are in the middle of the data. Unravelling the weights of the PCA axis show that elemental composition is a major dominating factor. Elementally, the thermoelectric effect seems to be dominated by the elements Sn, Ni, Zr in one side of the PCA, and elements Ge, Ge, and Ba on the other side of the PCA, with an overall negative relationship with O. This intuitively makes sense, as the band gap impacts the Seebeck coefficient, and the bandgap should be related to the metal ions, the semiconducting like elements, and overall should be hurt by oxides which kill conductivity. PCA has not made any great stride in interpretability of the data, and physical intuition had to be used to sanitize much of the data. A large open question is how the relationship between phases of elements, or specific crystal structures, impacts the thermoelectricity rather than the constituent elements.
